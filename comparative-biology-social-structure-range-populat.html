Comparative Biology, Social Structure, Range, Population, and Conservation Status of African and Asian Elephants

This report synthesises peer‑reviewed research, IUCN assessments, and recent census data to compare the biology, social organisation, geographic distribution, population trends, and conservation outlook of African (Loxodonta africana, Loxodonta cyclotis) and Asian (Elephas maximus) elephants.

African Elephant

African elephants are found in southern and central Africa (savanna and forest habitats); the forest elephant population, mostly in central Africa, comprises approximately a quarter of all African elephants.[4][6] Sources do not provide a total population figure for African elephants.

African elephants maintain complex, long‑term social relationships with many affiliates and form multi‑tiered social groups that are important for survival.[4] Forest elephants reproduce more slowly than savanna elephants, with a generation time of about 31 years, later age at first birth and longer intervals between calves.[4][6] This slower life history limits their ability to recover quickly from poaching losses, making them more vulnerable.[6]

Older IUCN assessments listed African elephants as Vulnerable.[4] A more recent reassessment treats forest and savanna elephants as separate species, classifying the forest elephant as Critically Endangered and the savanna elephant as Endangered.[4][6]

Asian Elephant

Asian elephants inhabit forest‑grassland mosaics in 13 South‑ and Southeast Asian countries.[5] They form fluid female‑centered groups of six to seven related individuals led by the oldest female, the matriarch; these groups may temporarily join others but herd sizes remain smaller than those of African savanna elephants and social ties are generally weaker.[5][7][8] Asian elephants spend much of their day feeding on grasses, tree bark, roots, leaves and cultivated crops such as bananas, rice and sugarcane, and need to drink fresh water at least once daily.[5][7]

Over 60 % of wild Asian elephants are found in India.[7] The global wild population was estimated at 48,323–51,680 individuals in 2019, i.e., fewer than 52,000.[8]

Asian elephants are listed as Endangered on the IUCN Red List, with the Sumatran subspecies classified as Critically Endangered.[5][7][8]
